# Startup Taste

Code is commodity. The only things that compound are **taste, distribution, and a primitive nobody else has.** Everything else is either in service of those three, or it's the bullshit — and your job is to catch it before it ships. Building stopped being the long pole around 2026: a product that took a quarter in 2021 takes a weekend now, so the scarce inputs are *what* to build and *getting people to care* — not the typing. The gates below all defend that shift.

## When to use
- Scoping anything, estimating timelines, or writing a roadmap / PRD / pitch.
- Someone asks "should we build X?", "is this a moat?", "what's our edge?"
- A plan smells like overkill: new framework, new abstraction, infra-first milestone, "make it configurable."
- **Skip it for:** pure execution of an already-scoped task (that's `writing-plans` → `batched-implementation`). Once the "should we?" is settled, **compound-v:brainstorming** takes over for the *how*.

## The master gate
**Does this grow taste, distribution, or a primitive?** None of the three → it's the bullshit; cut it or say why it's exempt. Run this first; most bad scope dies here.

**Suspect the safe-feeling default.** Inaction is a hidden risk that feels safe — it's often easier to do a hard thing that matters than an easy thing that doesn't. When choosing what to build, the comfortable low-stakes option is frequently the actual risk.

## The gates (run the relevant ones, name what you find)

This skill **owns** the scoping discipline the rest of the kit defers to: YAGNI / "every feature costs you forever" (name-the-cut), no premature machinery, de-risk the load-bearing assumption first, and verifier-first. **compound-v:brainstorming** and **compound-v:writing-plans** point here rather than re-deriving them.

**Estimate hygiene — hours/days for building, never weeks/months.**
If the bottleneck you name is "writing the code," re-estimate in hours, or move the time onto a *decision* or a *distribution* problem — that's where the real long pole now lives. A roadmap that budgets months of *building* is optimizing the part that got cheap.
Flag these strings whenever they're applied to construction (not to a decision or a distribution problem, where they may be honest): *"this will take weeks/months"*, *"multi-week"*, *"several months"*, *"a quarter to build."*

**Every scope names a cut.**
Subtraction is a first-class move — every feature costs you forever (Jobs cut Apple 350 products → 10; Granola cut half its features to expose the core interaction). A proposal that only *adds* (flags, endpoints, config, integrations) with zero removals is a feature factory, not a product call. Ask: "what did this remove?" If nothing, that's the finding. **Choose the cut per user, not per feature.** Low usage alone licenses nothing: twenty features at 4–5% each is the Word problem — "Everybody uses a different set of features." — and covering 80% of one user's workflow "delivers 0% of the value" to them. Count the users who would lose something they rely on, not the users of one feature. And every line you *keep* is a liability, not an asset — generation is cheap, ownership is the cost: each kept line must be maintained, ported, and paged on, and it eats the context window. An agent that generates cheaply and keeps everything is accruing debt at machine speed.

**No premature machinery — and the steps run in a strict order.**
**Make the requirement less dumb → delete the step → simplify → accelerate → automate.** The order is the load-bearing part: automating or accelerating a step you should have *deleted* is the classic mistake, and for an agent the corollary is sharper — never wrap a step in a skill, a subagent, or an abstraction before checking whether the step should exist at all. So don't install the manager-mode apparatus — abstractions, microservices, config systems, a plugin framework — before the *third* copy-paste forces it. "Make it configurable" as a reflex is flexibility theater: pick the opinionated default, remove the option. And treat novelty as a **countable budget**: roughly three novel-technology bets before operational complexity sinks you, so spend them on what makes the product different and pick boring, well-understood tools for everything else.
**Both directions are failures.** Don't over-engineer before you have users — and don't under-engineer after they arrive; the leanness rule expires the moment real load, real data, and real edge cases show up, and running it one-directionally ships fragility as "simplicity." Same for user requests: uncritically building what users ask for accretes crust, while over-indexing on your own taste ships what nobody asked for. The question is "does this request name a job the product already claims to do?" — not "do I trust my instincts?"

**Schlep ≠ overkill — cut ceremony, run toward the moat.**
"Cut the bullshit" is not "avoid hard work" — don't let one blur into the other. Ceremony-overkill (the premature abstraction above, restatement, process for its own sake) adds cost and no moat → cut it. A schlep is the unglamorous, hard-to-fake work — the data pipeline, the integration nobody wants to build, owning the ceiling layer, the 99.9%-reliability grind — that *is* the moat precisely because rivals won't stomach it. Run toward that. Test which one you're looking at: does skipping it make the product worse for the user, or just faster to ship? If skipping it only saves *you* effort and costs the *user* nothing, it was ceremony. If it's the thing a well-funded competitor would also have to grind through, it's the schlep — that's the work, do it. One discharge condition, though: a schlep is a moat only where it *compounds across customers*. Decagon's rule for forward-deployed work is that the output is not a one-off built for that customer — it lands in core product "so that the next 10 customers who ask about the same thing get it for free." Grind that never converts passes both tests above and still builds nothing; it is a consulting shop in a product's clothes. So ask of each schlep: does the second customer get it cheaper than the first? If not, it's revenue, not a moat — price it that way and say so.

**Wrapper test.**
Mentally swap the underlying model for the next release. Product basically unchanged, or the upgrade *kills* it → you built a wrapper (a feature), not a company. The upgrade should *help* you. The real bar: does this have architectural dependencies a well-funded competitor needs months to rebuild? (Reality check: the best consumer apps run **60–85% DAU/MAU**; the median gen-AI app sat near **14%** — a wrapper retains like a wrapper, and that gap is the tell.) The moat answer is never "our code" — code stopped being the proprietary asset. When someone says *"we could build that in a weekend,"* the rebuttal has a fixed shape: concede the demo, then enumerate integration depth — data access, per-customer tuning, guardrails, the long tail of edge cases. If the only moat on offer is the architecture, that's not a moat.

**Primitive in one sentence.**
You have a primitive only when you can state it in one sentence — "the search result, from links to a cited answer"; "speculative editing"; "the sync engine." Can't? You have a roadmap, not a primitive — stop and find it. The product is the *consequence* of the primitive (Figma built a WebGL renderer + multiplayer protocol for ~4 years; the design tool was then inevitable). "Existing workflow + LLM call" with no named core = a faster horse with a chat box.

**Contrarian insight + a timing leg — then check the provenance.**
A real opportunity has three parts: the conventional wisdom, a contrarian truth you believe that most don't, *and* a tech enabler that makes it doable **now**. If your insight leads you to the same architecture as the incumbents, it isn't contrarian enough — that's cosplay, not an edge. Most people with a contrarian take stop at part two; the timing leg is where theses die. So name the enabler: what changed recently (a model capability, a cost curve, a platform shift) that makes this buildable now and wasn't a year or two ago? No honest answer → either right-idea-wrong-decade, or there's no insight at all. The decisive bet usually looks irrational at the time — ask which of your "obviously wrong" ideas is wrong because it's *early*, not because it's *bad*. But **sequence** the ambition rather than betting it in one jump: layer the ambitious work on a stable base and defer the big-bang rewrite.
**Noticed, not brainstormed — and not a tar pit.** The best ideas are *noticed* by someone who has lived in a domain for years, not *produced* in a list-making session. Provenance is a tell independent of how contrarian the idea sounds: if it came from a brainstorm rather than a problem you keep bumping into, that's the finding. And some markets are graveyards — saturated, perennially attempted, historically resistant to solving (the calendar app, the universal inbox, the "Slack but better"); they look appealing precisely *because* the need is obvious, which is why everyone walks in and dies. In a tar pit the burden of proof is the timing leg above — what is finally different now — not enthusiasm.

**Persist on the goal, not the means.**
The scoping gates judge *starting*; this judges *continuing*. Persistence and obstinacy look identical from outside and split on one axis: the persistent are fixed on the goal and flexible on means, updating on evidence; the obstinate are fixed on the means, driven by ego. At a wall, ask whether the evidence still backs the goal — or only your pride does.

**Last mover, not first.**
Every moat test here is a moment-in-time test ("can a competitor rebuild it in months?"). Add the time axis: most of a company's value is cash flows years out, so durability dominates and growth is overrated — aim to be the *last* company in a category, not the first into it. For a 10x technical edge, also answer: why won't the *next* breakthrough supersede yours? An advantage anyone leapfrogs in two years helped users, not you. Pairs with the wrapper test — a moat that erodes on the next model release was never durable. Then ask the same question of the *problem*: is it persistent or temporary — does it decay as the user gets more competent, or as models improve? "Doesn't know the codebase" is temporary; "finding bugs" is persistent. If the problem decays, so does the revenue.

**Bounded beats unbounded.**
A bounded problem owned end-to-end (one narrow job, the whole workflow) is how a small team owns a category; an unbounded problem (general intelligence, "an assistant for everything") is how it wraps a model and competes with the lab. But be precise about *what* the moat is: a raw data flywheel is shaky — the next foundation model can erase the data advantage a startup accrues — while owning the **workflow and the user network** is durable. Capability is differentiation; defensibility is owning the end-to-end workflow and becoming the system of record. Prefer the bounded scope where you own that.

**Feature vs Product vs Company.**
Classify it. A feature does one thing and is cloned by next Tuesday (ships in days); a product solves a complete workflow (months); only a company compounds (data flywheel, distribution, lock-in). If it's a feature, name the company-level moat behind it — no moat → "wedge at best," say so.

**But moats come later — never let moat-forecasting decide whether to start.** Every gate above is sharpest as a check on *what* you're building and weakest as a reason not to build at all. A moat is inherently defensive: you need something worth defending before the question is even well-posed, so picking between two ideas by forecasting which has the bigger moat in five years is a named mistake — "that just isn't how it works." Solve one real customer's problem first; the moat categories turn up along the way. And when a moat does show up early for an AI startup it is usually execution-shaped — shipping speed, and process power over a surface rivals won't staff — not an architecture diagram.

**Revenue, not cost.**
Sell AI that grows the customer's revenue (no ceiling), not AI that cuts their cost (ceiling = the headcount you displaced, then you're done). Rewrite any pitch that leads with *"save time" / "cut costs" / "X% more efficient"* to lead with the outcome — leads found, deals closed, pipeline generated.

**One channel — but only after three cheap tests.**
Distribution is a third of the master gate, and at scoping time it reduces to one question: which channel is this plan's, and what is it explicitly not doing? Concentration of *effort* is the defensible claim — five channels at 20% each gets zero on all five — but concentration is the endpoint of a deliberate test, not the opening posture: run about three cheap tests in parallel first, and concentrate only once one of them looks like it could actually work. Do not claim most companies' first thousand users came through a single door; nothing counts that, and the figure that circulates for it traces to a vendor page with no study behind it. Directionality is fixed: products are built to fit channels, channels never mold to products; and the revenue model decides which channels are even available before a dollar is spent, so choose the pair together. You also don't need "users" — you need one **atomic network** that tips, the smallest group for which the product is already worth returning to. Applying escape-velocity tactics to a product whose first network hasn't tipped is the classic wrong-stage mistake. And most *"the product isn't ready"* diagnoses are really *"the message doesn't land"* — conversion is a language problem before it's a product problem, so test the words before you build features: copy is hours and a feature is days, which makes copy the cheaper experiment and therefore the one that runs first. **When distribution is the live question rather than a line in a scope, use compound-v:founder-distribution** — it owns the first-100 sequencing, the entry-friction gate, and the warrant grading behind every number here.

**Own the layer that sets your quality ceiling.**
Delete every third-party dependency on paper. If the core value dies, that dependency controls how good you're *allowed* to be — it's the layer you must eventually own. (Perplexity outgrew the Bing API and built its own index → near-zero URL overlap with competitors on identical queries; Cursor forked VS Code because the extension API made speculative edit impossible.) Label each external API "ceiling-setting" vs "commodity/swappable."

**De-risk the load-bearing assumption first.**
Order work by information gained ÷ time. Which single assumption, if false, makes the whole plan worthless? Test *that* first, with the cheapest experiment that resolves it. A plan whose first milestone is infra/scaffolding is fun-part-first — you're building on an untested belief. For an AI capability the cheapest experiment is almost always a *prompt*: validate the idea on a prompted frontier model before you fine-tune or collect data — never fine-tune to find out *whether* it works.

**Bound the upside before you build the apparatus.**
Name the best number the experiment could possibly return, *then* price the rig that would measure it — the flags, the extra shipped versions, the event instrumentation, the tables and queries and dashboard, and the dozen people across engineering, QA, analytics, PM and research who sit in the review. When the maximum achievable difference is worth less than the cost of measuring it, the experiment is a guaranteed loser however cheap the code is: settle it by judgment and spend the hours on a question whose answer could change what you build. Ask the metric's own prior question first — Slack's threads @-prefill was cut pre-release, came back six months later defended as research-backed, and the research was 2.17 messages per thread against 2.14, a result its founder recalls as not even clearing significance and priced at thousands of person-hours; but before any of that, *why* would a longer thread be better? A shorter one may be. This does not discharge verifier-first below — an eval decides whether you can drive quality at all, which is not the same question as arbitrating one variant.

**Verifier-first for AI.**
Before building an AI feature, name the verifiable signal / eval. No auto-check → you can't drive quality, and *no eval system is the #1 cause of failed AI products.* Build the verifier before the feature; "we'll eyeball quality" is the failure mode, not a plan. (Quality + verifiability beat quantity: 4,000 good verifiable examples beat 4M low-quality ones.) Once you commit to building, **compound-v:evals** is how you actually construct that signal — error analysis, binary judge, align-to-human. (Note: AI that automates a motion only amplifies whatever you feed it — an SDR that scales a script that doesn't close just automates the broken version at volume. Validate the loop before you scale it.) Verifiability is also a problem-*selection* filter, not just a prerequisite: prefer the wedge whose feedback loop is fastest — where checking is cheap (code, math) agents go superhuman; where it isn't, expect only marginal gains (Cursor left CAD for code precisely because the loop was instant — "run the code, see if it works").

**Harness before model.**
When an AI system underperforms, the fix is almost never a bigger model — it's context, tools, error recovery. Same model, different harness = real swings (v0 took one model to error-free via four engineering layers — a large jump with no model upgrade). Challenge *"upgrade to a bigger model" / "wait for the next model"*: have you exhausted context, tool design, and the verify-retry loop first? And prune the other way as the model improves: a harness patch built to cover a past model's weakness is deletable scaffolding, not permanent architecture — Claude Code's own team cut a nudge-hack and ~20% of its system prompt once a new model stopped needing it. Delete the old workaround before adding the next one.

**Reliability before capability.**
A reliable-but-narrower feature beats a more-capable-but-flaky one once users get burned: a feature that fails often enough to notice teaches the user not to trust it, and that lesson sticks. The impressive-demo, unreliable-in-practice trade is usually the wrong one: pick the boring thing that always works over the magic thing that mostly works. Reliability is *threshold-shaped*, not linear: below the bar users rationally abandon and no habit forms — you have a demo, not a product; cross it and the tool flips from experimental to relied-on, and in a new category the first team over the bar tends to take it. And measure it on the **messy real distribution, never the demo** — the demo is a cherry-pick; if it doesn't hold on the 50K-line monorepo with bad names and inconsistent style, it isn't working.

**PMF is a number, not a vibe.**
"Users seem to like it" is not product-market fit. The field-standard read: survey users with "how would you feel if you could no longer use this?" and build for the segment that answers *very disappointed* — when a large enough share answers that way, you have a fit signal, not just enthusiasm. Track **paid power-user retention**, not signups or MAU — and write it as a predicate somebody either clears or doesn't, not as a category. Cursor's was literal: "are you using the AI four or five days a week out of seven days a week," and explicitly "not DAUs, MAUs or anything like that." If you cannot state your version as a frequency and a payment status, you have renamed the vibe rather than replaced it. No segment clears the bar → you have interest, not fit; keep finding the people who'd be gutted to lose it rather than widening to please everyone. And hunt disconfirmation at the right address: interrogate the people who did **not** buy — the churned, the trial that stalled, the deal that went elsewhere — not your fans. Fans can only tell you why the thesis holds; non-buyers are the only ones who know what breaks it (**compound-v:critical-thinking**).

**A number went up ≠ better.**
A moving metric is not proof of value (Goodhart). Bolt Tetris onto anything and engagement rises — it doesn't mean the product improved; engagement can just redistribute activity or measure friction. Before celebrating a number, name the *user outcome* it's a proxy for and check that outcome moved too. Popularity, anecdotes ("my cousin loved it"), a competitor having it, the boss's preference, and "someone else will build it" are bad reasons dressed as evidence — run the master gate regardless of who's asking or what spiked.

## Refusal templates (use the shape, fill the specifics)
- **Bullshit / over-scope:** "This grows none of taste, distribution, or the primitive — and it only adds. Cutting it (or tell me which of the three it serves)."
- **Wrapper:** "Swap the model for the next release and this is unchanged / dies. That's a feature, not a moat. The company-level lock-in behind it would be ___ — do we have it?"
- **Weeks/months for code:** "Building isn't the long pole anymore. The hard part here is the *decision* about ___ — let's spend the time there and ship the build in hours/days."
- **No eval:** "There's no verifiable signal yet, so we can't drive quality. Let's define the eval before writing the feature."
- **Correcting an overstatement (the default shape of a correction):** affirm the direction, name the literal overreach, substitute the corrected version — *"directionally right, literally overstated; the honest version is ___."* Use it instead of the two failure modes it sits between: agreeing wholesale, or contradicting wholesale.

## What this skill is NOT
It is not a fan. A response that flatters every claim *is* the bullshit it's trying to remove. Name the violated property; offer the rewrite; assume a high-agency operator who already ships, so cut hand-holding, not rigor.
It is also not a substitute for judgment, and the strongest attack on any skill kit is the true one: hiring and judgment determine product quality far more than process, tooling, or design reviews — you cannot spec quality into existence, only execution delivers it. The resolution is narrow. What survives of process is the **artifact a human reads instead of the diff** — the named cut, the one-sentence primitive, the eval, the channel. Cut the ceremony, keep the artifact.
